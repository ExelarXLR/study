## U1. Written communication rubric

Original: `Rubric_WrittenCommunication.pdf`, 1 page.

The four dimensions are **Content & Development**, **Organization**, **Language**, and **Writing**. The scale runs 1-5, with descriptions principally at 1, 3, and 5 and intermediate performance indicated at 2 and 4.

The strongest descriptions emphasize a clearly stated and developed thesis; accurate, specific detail appropriate to the audience; a coherent central theme; purposeful, precise language and sentence structure; and clear, consistent, complete documentation integrated into the work. Use it to check written explanations, handouts, slide language, and reference practices where those are assessed. It is not itself a slide-design specification.

## U3. Jacobs and Hyman (2010)

Original: `Jacobs%2C+Hyman+-+2010+-+15+Strategies+for+Giving+Oral+Presentations.pdf`, 4 pages. Title: *15 Strategies for Giving Oral Presentations*. Authors: Lynn F. Jacobs and Jeremy S. Hyman. Printed date: February 24, 2010.

The source's sequence is retained below; explanations are paraphrases:

1. **Do your homework:** prepare seriously; professor/TA feedback can help.
2. **Play the parts:** organize around a few main sections and signpost them.
3. **Do a dry run:** rehearse for timing and identify sticking points, potentially with a listener.
4. **Look presentable:** use appearance appropriate to the occasion.
5. **Talk; don't read:** deliver the main ideas naturally from notes rather than burying yourself in a script.
6. **Take it slow:** the audience is less familiar with the topic; trim or summarize rather than rushing.
7. **Use aids:** explain slides, handouts, or board material rather than assuming they are self-explanatory.
8. **Don't bury the crowd:** avoid overwhelming quotations and data.
9. **Be yourself:** favor an authentic manner.
10. **Play it straight:** humor should not displace the academic purpose.
11. **Circle the crowd:** connect with people across the room through eye contact and shared space.
12. **Appear relaxed:** pauses and water can help presentation manner, even when the speaker feels nervous.
13. **Finish strong:** make the audience's new understanding explicit.
14. **Welcome interruptions:** questions can indicate engagement; respond briefly when appropriate.
15. **Know when to stop lecturing:** during discussion, listen and address the actual question rather than delivering unrelated prepared content.

These are the authors' practical recommendations, not fifteen independently established experimental findings. Their opening claims about public-speaking fear are not used as verified prevalence statistics in this system.

## U4. Matthew O. Jackson (1998)

Original: `Jackson+-+1998+-+Notes+on+Presenting+a+Paper.pdf`, 6 pages. Title: *Notes on Presenting a Paper*. Social Sciences 212a, Fall 1998. Jackson explicitly presents the advice as his own tastes and experience for seminar-style talks.

His outline, in order:

1. **The Problem to be Studied:** explain the question, why it matters, and what the audience will learn (p. 1).
2. **The Contributions of this Paper:** describe the relevant model, approach, data/design, and results; consider positive, normative, or methodological contributions and applicability beyond the setting (pp. 1-2).
3. **Relation to the Literature:** compare the closest work concisely rather than giving a broad survey. He permits reversing items 2 and 3 when a direct extension makes that clearer (pp. 2-3).
4. **Definitions, Descriptions of Data, Etc.:** introduce the framework simply, use intelligible notation, and use examples/special cases that convey the insight (p. 3).
5. **Main Results:** explain why the result occurs, not merely what the theorem/table says. Emphasize informative proof steps and digestible findings; label axes and variables. A class focused on proofs or methods may need the full derivation (pp. 4-5).
6. **Conclusions:** summarize the learning, applications, limitations, and further questions (p. 5).

His final remarks prioritize organization and clarity over flair; fit the amount of material to the time; do not reproduce every research dead end; remain accessible to a mixed audience; manage diversions while preserving time for discussion (pp. 5-6). His advice about providing a roadmap and revealing conclusions early is especially relevant to technical economics. It should not be erased by a generic storytelling formula.

It does not establish that the underlying claims are correct or that a word-for-word script is the best delivery method.

**Checks before reuse:** verify its equations, definitions, equilibrium/uniqueness claims, comparative statics, historical statistics, historical causal explanations, and policy implications against the research paper and appropriate evidence. The timing map reaches minute 30 before opening discussion, so it does not visibly reserve a substantive separate Q&A block. Confirm whether the actual assignment includes Q&A within its limit. Do not assume this is the user's first presentation topic.
