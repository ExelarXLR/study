# Presentation learning: outside research and practice guidance

Sources checked September 9, 2026. These sources supplement, rather than replace, the uploaded course rubrics and presentation readings. Summaries below separate experiments from professional guidance and our proposed application. The complete workflow in PRESENTATIONS.md has not itself been experimentally validated.

## R1. Retrieval rather than familiarity alone

Roediger, H. L., and Karpicke, J. D. (2006). *Test-Enhanced Learning*. Psychological Science, 17(3), 249-255. DOI: 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x.

Primary publication: https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x/

**Experiment:** after studying prose, repeated recall produced better delayed retention than repeated studying, while studying could look better on the immediate test and increase confidence. **Application:** explain the argument with notes closed, then check accuracy. **Boundary:** this does not show that reading is unnecessary or directly measure public-speaking skill. Do not equate a smooth rereading experience with durable mastery.

## R2. Retrieval can support conceptual learning

Karpicke, J. D., and Blunt, J. R. (2011). *Retrieval Practice Produces More Learning than Elaborative Studying with Concept Mapping*. Science, 331, 772-775. DOI: 10.1126/science.1199327.

Author-hosted paper: https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2011/2011_Karpicke_Blunt_Science.pdf

**Experiments:** retrieval practice outperformed the studied concept-mapping conditions on subsequent learning measures, including comprehension and inference. **Application:** reconstruct a mechanism and answer a changed-case question; use the map to diagnose gaps, not only to reread. **Boundary:** the result concerns particular tasks and conditions, not proof that all diagrams or all concept mapping are inferior. Transfer to presentation preparation is an application of the evidence, not the original outcome tested.

## R3. Distribute later practice

Cepeda, N. J., Vul, E., Rohrer, D., Wixted, J. T., and Pashler, H. (2008). *Spacing Effects in Learning: A Temporal Ridgeline of Optimal Retention*. Psychological Science, 19(11), 1095-1102. DOI: 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x.

University repository: https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0kp5q19x

**Experiment:** the useful gap between learning occasions depended on the delay before the final test; longer gaps were not invariably better. **Application:** revisit difficult mechanisms after intervening work and on later days when the deadline permits. **Boundary:** there is no source-supported universal '1-3-7 day' schedule for every learner, subject, or talk. Timing should respond to demonstrated forgetting and the actual deadline.

## R4. TEDx Speaker Guide

Official guide: https://storage.ted.com/tedx/manuals/tedx_speaker_guide.pdf

**Professional guidance, not an experiment:** develop a clear idea, fact-check it, organize evidence, create useful rather than obligatory slides, and rehearse aloud with feedback and timing. Stories and emotion serve communication of the idea. **Application:** use a meaningful opening and practice the actual delivery, not only a silent read. **Boundary:** the guide's under-18-minute format and styling recommendations do not override a professor's duration, roadmap, proof, reference, or discussion requirements.

## R5. Assertion-evidence slide design

Penn State engineering communication materials, *Guidelines for Assertion-Evidence Slides*, drawing on Michael Alley, *The Craft of Scientific Presentations*.

Primary teaching document: https://www.writing.engr.psu.edu/guidelines_AE_slides.pdf

**Professional guidance:** begin explanatory body slides with a sentence making the point and support it with visual evidence rather than a dense list. **Application:** make the slide title communicate the actual, properly qualified finding and use the body to help explain it. **Boundary:** this handout is a design recommendation, not by itself an experimental demonstration of learning gains. Title, orientation, discussion, and reference slides may need a different form.

## Advice that needs reconciliation, not silent blending

**Roadmaps:** Jackson explicitly favors giving a technical audience its destination; the TEDx guide favors unobtrusive structure. Use a brief explicit roadmap for a seminar and natural transitions for a general audience.

**Movement:** Jacobs/Hyman discuss sharing the room and connecting with the audience; the TEDx guide cautions against unpurposeful pacing. Our synthesis is purposeful movement plus stable delivery, not a rigid mandate to walk or remain motionless.

**Notes:** the supplied example uses complete scripts, while Jacobs/Hyman discourage reading to the room. A script can be a preparation scaffold; cues can be the live surface. Neither should substitute for understanding.

**Detail:** accessible explanations do not justify omitting required mathematics, identification, or evidence. Jackson explicitly allows course-specific technical depth.

## Proposed tactics versus established results

The three-depth explanation ladder, five-question diagnostic, three rehearsal passes, short practice-block timings, audience-prediction moments, and post-talk one-improvement log are our practical design choices. Test their usefulness against actual understanding, timing, audience feedback, and preparation cost. They are not presented as neuroscience facts or as a proven optimal package.

Keep future additions selective: prefer a primary paper or official teaching guide, state its population/task and limits, and identify the specific decision it changes. Do not turn preparation into collecting unlimited advice.
