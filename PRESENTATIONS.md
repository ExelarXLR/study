# Presentations: understand, explain, defend, deliver

## Purpose and status

Build economics knowledge and transferable speaking ability with the least wasted preparation. The target is not to sound intelligent: it is to make a difficult idea accurate, clear, useful, and memorable to another person.

**Status:** reusable preparation/coaching system established September 9, 2026. First assignment details are still pending. The uploaded Mitra speaker notes are reference material, not confirmation of the first presentation's topic.

This extends [STUDY_SYSTEM.md](STUDY_SYSTEM.md), not a separate planner. Use one [presentation brief](templates/presentation.md) per actual assignment. Consult [the uploaded-material notes](resources/presentations/USER_MATERIALS.md) and [outside research](resources/presentations/RESEARCH.md) only when needed.

**Evidence boundary:** the workflow below is a proposed synthesis. Research supports particular components, not a guaranteed transformation into a TED-level speaker or an optimal number of preparation minutes. Instructor requirements and course AI rules take precedence.

## The operating bargain

The assistant handles organization, source navigation, comparison, formatting, and permitted drafting. The learner does the irreplaceable work: reconstructing explanations, testing assumptions, making judgments, and speaking aloud. Reduce clerical effort, not the thinking and practice that create competence.

Begin with the exact assignment, audience, duration, whether Q&A is inside the time limit, group responsibilities, deadline, sources, and AI policy. Read [COURSES.md](COURSES.md); do not transfer permission from one course to another. Its recorded Econometrics restrictions, for example, allow general study help rather than AI-produced graded deliverables. Confirm the current policy for the actual assignment before drafting assessed content.

## One reusable preparation loop

### 1. Diagnose before producing slides

Ask one question at a time and wait for the learner's attempt:

- What is the question, and why does it matter?
- What is the main answer in everyday language?
- Why should that answer follow: what is the mechanism?
- What evidence or reasoning supports it?
- What assumption, limitation, or alternative explanation matters most?

An incomplete answer is a location to repair, not a reason to start a long generic lecture. Diagnose vocabulary, mechanism, math, graph, evidence, memory, or delivery separately. Explain the missing piece briefly, then have the learner reconstruct it. Revisit it after another topic. Retrieval research motivates this approach [R1-R3 in RESEARCH.md]; the exact five-question diagnostic is our coaching design.

### 2. Build one argument map

Use a single chain: **question -> stakes -> central answer -> mechanism -> evidence -> limitation -> implication**.

Record at most three essential audience takeaways as a starting default, not a universal law. For every major claim, retain its source and page/section, scope, and verification status. Distinguish the author's conclusion from our interpretation, an illustrative analogy, and outside evidence. Do not let a polished summary become a substitute for checking the source.

An economics presentation should make the reader/listener understand the economic actors, incentives, constraints, adjustment process, outcome, and who gains or loses. For empirical work, add what is measured, the comparison/design, identification assumptions, uncertainty, and practical importance. For theory, separate a result conditional on assumptions from an empirical claim about the world. A model prediction alone does not establish that a historical mechanism actually occurred.

Use Jackson's research-paper framework as the academic backbone [U4]. Answer what the paper contributes and why its approach is suited to the question. Do not force labels such as theoretical, econometric, empirical, and experimental into a single exclusive box; a paper may combine methods. Likewise, discuss positive, normative, and methodological contributions where relevant rather than listing labels without explanations.

### 3. Learn each difficult idea at three depths

For each essential idea, the learner should be able to give:

1. A short everyday explanation without unexplained jargon.
2. A mechanism explanation using a concrete example and, where useful, a graph or equation.
3. A defensible technical answer explaining assumptions, evidence, limitations, and what changes in a new example.

Illustrative drill lengths are 20 seconds, 90 seconds, and 3 minutes; adapt them to the concept. These are training constraints, not scientifically established optimum durations. Start with a worked example when needed, then remove scaffolding. Include a new case or changed assumption so success is more than reciting a familiar sentence.

**Graph protocol:** name axes, units, population/time frame, baseline, what changes, why the curve moves or the optimum changes, the resulting implication, and what cannot be inferred. **Equation protocol:** say the relationship in English, define symbols and units, work a simple case, then interpret the economics. Never hide required analysis just to make the talk feel like a popular lecture. Jackson explicitly makes room for detailed proofs when the class requires them [U4].

### 4. Design for the audience's questions

Use the uploaded webarchive's audience questions as a comprehension audit [U6]. At each section ask: Why care? What does this mean? Why would that follow? How do we know? What could be wrong? What changes because of it?

For an academic talk, give the main result and a short roadmap early; do not withhold the conclusion for suspense [U4]. For a general-interest talk, compress formal signposting into natural transitions while keeping the evidence intact [R4]. This is a deliberate adaptation between two different styles, not a claim that their advice is identical.

Build one low-friction participation moment around an important idea: a prediction before revealing a result, a quick choice between explanations, or an invitation to apply the mechanism to a new case. Give thinking time and explain the answer without embarrassing anyone. Participation is useful only when it serves the learning objective; do not add games for their own sake. These interaction choices are coaching proposals, not an experimentally validated engagement formula.

### 5. Turn the argument into visuals and speaking cues

Only now create the deck, where course policy permits it. The argument map supplies the slides, notes, Q&A preparation, and later review questions. Avoid maintaining a full essay, separate script, and unrelated slide outline that drift apart.

For explanatory slides, prefer a sentence stating the point and a visual that actually supports it; cite evidence in a legible location and retain full references in notes/appendix [R5]. For example, a useful headline states the specific finding rather than merely saying 'Results.' Preserve uncertainty in the headline. Do not manufacture a graph, source, quotation, or numerical result.

Use a few cue phrases, the key evidence/example, a transition, and a time target in speaker notes. A full script may help clarify an opening, closing, or exact technical statement during preparation; it is not the default live-reading surface. The supplied speaker notes illustrate timing, transitions, and technical Q&A, but their economic content remains unverified [U5].

Give the audience a simple main path and keep genuinely useful technical details in backup slides. Use consistent notation, readable type, labeled units, and accessible contrast; do not rely on color alone. Test legibility on the actual device/projector. Build visuals for explanation, not decoration.

### 6. Rehearse to repair the weakest link

Start speaking before the slides are beautiful. Use three passes as a practical default:

- **Understanding pass:** explain the argument with no deck. Repair the first place the explanation stops making sense.
- **Timed audience pass:** use the actual cues and visuals. Record when a new listener would get lost. Ask a listener to explain the main mechanism back, not just whether the talk was good.
- **Pressure pass:** rehearse questions, an interruption, a blank moment, a shortened version, and any group handoffs. Rehearse under realistic equipment/time conditions when possible [R4].

A recording can be useful. Review only one or two dimensions at a time: for example, pace and clarity, or eye contact and purposeful pauses. Text alone supports critique of structure and wording, not claims about the speaker's actual posture, eye contact, or vocal performance. Do not pretend those were observed.

If the talk overruns, remove optional content instead of racing through it [U3/U4]. Keep Q&A time explicit. Agree on mandatory versus optional sections and a recovery line such as: 'The essential point is ...'. This is a recovery tool, not permission to bluff.

**Q&A pattern:** listen -> clarify if needed -> answer the precise question -> give one reason or piece of evidence -> state the boundary -> stop. Practice a basic peer question, a professor's mechanism question, and a strong skeptical objection. When a fact is unknown, distinguish what is established from what would need checking. Do not invent an answer to protect an appearance of confidence.

## A low-friction session

When time is tight, use approximately 5 minutes of cold explanation, 10 minutes repairing one high-value gap, 10 minutes teaching it aloud, and 5 minutes on a transfer question and the next action. This 30-minute block is a planning heuristic, not a promise that an unfamiliar paper or a complete presentation can be mastered in half an hour.

Protect the order: accuracy and understanding -> understandable structure -> at least one timed run -> visual polish. Allocate additional preparation to demonstrated weaknesses, not endless rewriting of material already understood. Short later retrieval sessions are preferred to treating one long sitting as the whole learning process [R1-R3].

## Readiness: observable evidence, not confidence alone

Ready for a specific talk means the learner can explain the thesis without reading, reconstruct the central mechanism, interpret the key evidence, name an important limitation, answer a new application or counterexample, and deliver the actual talk within its allotted time. Confirm required attribution, group handoffs, and technical backup. A delayed check is still needed before labeling the subject durably mastered.

Assess subject knowledge, organization, delivery, audience connection, and aid usefulness using the supplied oral rubric [U2]. **Its technology row appears reversed; do not assign a numeric score for that row until its direction is confirmed.** The written rubric adds checks for thesis development, coherence, purposeful language, and documentation [U1]. Neither a beautiful deck nor self-reported confidence proves mastery.

After each presentation, save only: one recurring knowledge gap, one delivery improvement, relevant instructor feedback, and one concrete next drill. Add demonstrated concept changes to MASTERY.md only after actual learner evidence. Do not archive every rehearsal or create another administrative burden.
